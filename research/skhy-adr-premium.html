How long can the SKHY premium last?

SK hynix's Nasdaq ADSs trade roughly a third above the same shares in Seoul. The gap is not a pricing error waiting on arbitrage. It is the price of a wrapper that cannot be manufactured, and history says such premiums end when the issuer adds supply, not when traders notice them.

The answer in five points

  1. A 30–40% premium is stable for as long as the conversion constraint is left in place. Restricted depositary receipts have held 30–60% premiums for one and a half years (TSMC, late 1998 to 2000) and for six and a half years (Infosys, March 1999 to autumn 2005). Nothing in the arbitrage mechanism forces convergence on a timetable.
  2. Premiums of this kind end through supply, not through arbitrage. In both large historical compressions the decisive events were issuer-driven: TSMC's conversion-sale programmes and its June 2000 ADS offering, and Infosys's three sponsored secondary ADS offerings in 2003, 2005 and 2006. Demand busts (2000–01) and market liberalisation (Korea 1998, India 2002, Taiwan 2003) shrank the steady-state premium, but they finished the job only in combination with more DRs.
  3. For SKHY the constraint is corporate and regulatory, not procedural. The DR quota is 2.5% of shares and was fully used at listing. New ADSs can only be created against ADSs that have been cancelled, and nobody cancels a security trading at a 36% premium. Raising the quota requires a board resolution, a Korean FSC registration and an SEC registration: a follow-on offering in all but name. The 90-day lockup rules that out before early October.
  4. The premium's half-life is therefore a decision by SK hynix and the FSC, not a market process. Issuing more ADSs at a premium is the value-maximising move for the company and its Korean shareholders (TSMC did exactly this in June 2000 at about a 43% premium). Until that decision is taken, the demand calendar (Nasdaq-100 eligibility in December, ETF wrappers, retail on both sides of the Pacific) points the other way.
  5. Size the risk with the right arithmetic. If the premium goes to zero with Seoul unchanged, SKHY falls 26.5%, not 36%. A compression to 10% is a 19% fall. Each 2.5% tranche of new ADSs would double the float; the first such tranche is the event that would move the premium into the 10–20% band TSMC has lived in for most of the last twenty years.

Definition and arithmetic

premium = (SKHY price × 10) / (KRX 000660 price ÷ USD/KRW) − 1

Ten ADSs represent one common share. The Seoul leg is converted at the prevailing won rate, so the premium is a dollar comparison and the ADR holder implicitly carries won exposure. The Hyperliquid perps used for the live series track these two prices through deployer oracles: xyz:SKHX is the dollar value of one Seoul share, xyz:SKHY is the ADS.

The unwind is asymmetric. A premium of P on the ADR means the ADR must fall by P/(1+P) to reach parity, not by P.

What the series says

The premium did not decay after 29 July when two-way conversion opened; it rose to its 42% high two weeks later. That is the expected result of a headroom rule with no headroom: the conversion right is real but nobody will exercise it at a premium, so supply stays at 177.9M ADSs. The premium behaves like a scarce security's own price, moving with US flow and sentiment rather than with the Korean share.

Volatility is the second barrier. With both legs near 120% annualised and the premium itself swinging 3 percentage points a day, a short-ADR long-Seoul position that entered at 30% in late July was 12 points under water by mid-August. Lamont's July note put Seoul above 100% and the ADS above 200% realised volatility. Arbitrage capital with a convergence date it cannot name and path risk of that size stays small, which is why 15% short interest has not closed the gap.

Why arbitrage cannot close it

The textbook trade is buy Seoul, deposit, sell ADSs. Every step after "buy Seoul" is blocked or expensive:

  • Deposit is one-way. ADSs convert into Seoul shares freely; Seoul shares convert into ADSs only against cancelled ADSs. Korean deposit agreements have long required issuer consent for deposits (subject to government authorisation), with re-deposits of withdrawn shares the standing exception; LG Display's 20-F states the pattern explicitly. SKHY's F-1 goes further and warns that even re-deposit may be refused.
  • The cap is a legal artefact, not a queue. 2.5% was set by the board to keep SK square at or above 20% under the Fair Trade Act. More new shares in ADS form dilute SK square unless it buys Seoul shares alongside. Converting existing shares (the TSMC and Infosys route) avoids dilution but still needs a board resolution, an FSC registration statement and an SEC registration.
  • Timing. The company and affiliates are locked up for 90 days. Even a willing board cannot add supply before roughly 7 October.
  • The synthetic version is costly. Short SKHY (borrow, roughly 15% of ADSs already lent), long 000660 through a Korean custody line (open to institutions; foreign-investor registration IDs were abolished in December 2023 and omnibus accounts and 24-hour won trading arrived in 2024), hedge the won, and wait for a convergence event that no one can date. Lamont's arithmetic for TSMC applies: a 1% annual carry against a 20% premium that takes two decades to close is a losing trade.
  • Demand keeps arriving. Nasdaq-100 eligibility from the December 2026 reconstitution (if SKHY ranks by ADS market value after the seasoning period), ETF wrappers, and retail in two countries. TSMC's premium widened, not narrowed, into the 2024 AI rally for the same reason.

The Frankfurt test. SK hynix has a GDR in Frankfurt that trades at parity. Same company, same Korean regulator, same fundamentals. The 36% is not about SK hynix or about Korea; it is about a specific US-listed security whose supply is fixed by decree while its demand is not.

What history says about duration

Two regimes. Where the home share and the receipt are fungible in both directions, premiums live inside transaction costs (most European and Japanese cross-listings, Samsung's London GDR after Korea removed ownership limits in May 1998, SK hynix's own GDR). Where creation is restricted, the receipt is a separate security and its premium is set by demand against a fixed float.

Three channels, in order of importance

Supply. Every compression from above 30% coincides with the issuer letting more DRs exist. TSMC's board adopted its conversion-sale policy in May 1999 and sold ADSs into the premium in June 2000; the premium never regained its 2000 levels even in 2021 or 2024, when demand was again extreme. Infosys's premium stayed above 30% for six years because India's headroom rule locked supply; it came down within eighteen months of two sponsored offerings totalling $2.7bn. Note the level at which supply mattered: Infosys still carried a 36–50% premium with ADSs at 14% of shares. Float share alone does not end a premium; the change in supply relative to demand does.

Demand. The 2000–01 bust cut TSMC's premium from over 100% to the 40s within months, but with the float still small the premium stayed positive for years. A demand shock is fast but partial.

Access. Removing ownership limits (Korea 1998), abolishing QFII (Taiwan 2003) and two-way fungibility (India 2002) each lowered the steady-state premium by widening who can hold the home share. They act slowly and on the institutional part of demand only. Retail and index demand are not touched by them.

Scenarios for SKHY

A. Constraint holds, demand holds (the Infosys path)

No board action; index inclusion in December adds forced buyers. Premium oscillates in a 20–50% band with a 30–40% centre for as long as the memory cycle and US retail enthusiasm last. This is the base case until a supply event is announced. Its risk is not a slow bleed but a step change on the day one is announced.

B. Managed supply (the TSMC path)

After the lockup, SK hynix or its large holders arrange sponsored conversions or a follow-on ADS offering at a premium. Each 2.5% tranche doubles the float. Expect a step down toward 10–20% on announcement (an 12–19% ADR move against Seoul), then a TSMC-style residual premium set by the one-way rule and index demand. Signals: a board resolution, a Korean securities registration statement, an SEC F-3 or F-1, a depositary notice, or SK square buying Seoul shares to preserve its 20%.

C. Demand bust (the 2000 path)

A memory-cycle or AI-sentiment reversal removes the marginal US buyer. Premium compresses quickly toward 10–20% but, with float unchanged, is unlikely to reach parity. Note that in this scenario both legs fall and the ADR falls more.

D. Access liberalisation (the Samsung path)

Korea's push for MSCI developed-market status keeps making Seoul easier for institutions. This trims the "restricted institution" bid over years, not months, and does nothing to retail or index demand. Relevant to the terminal level, not to timing.

Calendar. ~7 Oct 2026: lockup expiry, the first date on which supply can legally be added. Late Nov 2026: Nasdaq-100 reference date; announcement early Dec, effective third Friday of December. Bi-monthly: Nasdaq short-interest reports. Continuous: KSD conversion data (any cancellation creates headroom; any headroom used is a signal that the premium is being arbitraged).

What to monitor

On the dashboard

  • Mark vs oracle premium. The oracle premium is the real-world gap; the mark premium is what Hyperliquid's 24-hour books expect it to be. A mark premium persistently below the oracle premium means the synthetic market is pricing compression.
  • Funding differential. The xyz:SKHX perp has been trading 0.3–1% under its oracle with strongly negative funding, so the synthetic market leans short the Seoul leg. The dashboard's carry tile shows what a long-Seoul, short-ADS perp position earns or pays per hour.
  • Range statistics. A move outside the 24–42% band since 15 July is the first thing worth explaining.

Off the dashboard

  • KSD DR conversion statistics and Citibank programme data (ADSs outstanding, cancellations).
  • SK hynix 6-K filings and board resolutions; any Korean securities registration statement for DRs; SEC F-3/F-1 filings.
  • Nasdaq short-interest reports and borrow fees for SKHY; SKHZ (short ETF) assets.
  • Korean retail net purchases (KSD SEIBro overseas-stock data); ETF launches; index committee announcements.
  • SK square's stake relative to the 20% floor; National Pension Service and foreign-institution holdings that could be offered in a sponsored conversion.
